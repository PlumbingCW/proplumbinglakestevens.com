A faucet that will not shut off

A faucet that will not shut off is an emergency when the valve underneath also fails. If the sink fills or a supply line has burst, close the shutoff valves under the sink. If those are stuck or leak, shut off the main and call our 24/7 line.

A supply line that has burst can flood a cabinet in minutes. Look for a bulging braided hose, water in the cabinet and corroded connections. These lines are cheap to replace but costly to ignore.

Fixture problems and what causes them

  • Dripping spout: a worn cartridge, washer or seat.
  • Leak at the handle: a failing O-ring or packing nut.
  • Low flow: a clogged aerator or cartridge.
  • Shower valve leak or showerhead drip: a worn cartridge or a failed seal behind the wall.
  • Tub faucet that will not shut off: a worn diverter or cartridge.
  • Stuck shutoff valve: old compression valves that seize.

Shower valves and tub faucets

A leaking shower valve is harder to fix than a kitchen faucet because the valve sits inside the wall. Most are repaired by replacing the cartridge from the front, with no wall work. A valve body that has corroded or cracked may need access from the back.

Burst or broken supply valves under sinks are common in older homes where the stop valves have not moved in decades. We replace them with quarter-turn valves, which close fully and are easy to find in an emergency.

Local note

Fixtures and Lake Stevens water

Snohomish County PUD lists total hardness between 9 and 89 ppm for the Lake Stevens system, averaging 73. Water in that range leaves fewer mineral crusts on aerators and cartridges than very hard water does. If your aerator still clogs, that is more likely sediment from a recent water line event than hardness.

Many Lake Stevens homes date from the post-2000 growth years, with modern single-handle fixtures that use ceramic cartridges. Those cartridges are cheap to replace and last for years, so a leaking faucet in a newer house is usually a quick cartridge swap.

What a faucet repair visit looks like

  1. Shut off waterWe close the stops and test that they hold.
  2. DiagnoseWe pull the handle and inspect the cartridge, seat and O-rings.
  3. Repair or replaceWe fix what is worn or recommend a new faucet if it is past saving.
  4. TestWe run the fixture, check for leaks and show you the shutoffs.

Standards for faucet parts

Replacement faucets and parts should be listed to recognized plumbing standards, and the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), adopted in Washington under WAC 51-56, requires listed fixtures and fittings. Buy from a reputable supplier, and match the faucet to your sink hole count before you order.

Questions we hear

Do you handle faucet emergencies at night or on weekends in Lake Stevens?

Yes. Our dispatch line answers around the clock. If a line has burst, close the shutoffs under the sink or the main and call.

Why is my faucet dripping?

A worn cartridge, washer or seat is the usual cause. We replace the part and test the fixture.

Can you fix a leaking shower valve?

Yes. Most are fixed by swapping the cartridge, and some need access from behind the wall.

Do you replace old shutoff valves?

Yes. Replacing stuck stops with quarter-turn valves is quick and prevents trouble in an emergency.

Is it worth repairing an old faucet?

If parts are available and the finish is sound, yes. If it is corroded or discontinued, replacement is better.
