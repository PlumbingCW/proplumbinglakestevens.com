A cable pokes a hole through a clog. A hydro jetter scours the whole pipe wall with high-pressure water. When a line keeps backing up, jetting is usually the fix that lasts.

When a sewer backup calls for hydro jetting

Hydro jetting pushes water through a hose with a nozzle that fires jets forward and backward. The spray cuts grease, scrapes scale and flushes silt toward the main. It cleans the pipe back to something close to its original diameter, which a cable cannot do.

A backup that returns every few months is the classic jetting case. So is a line coated with grease from a kitchen, or one narrowed by years of mineral and soap buildup. If water is already backing into a tub or floor drain, call the 24/7 line and stop using water until we arrive.

Backup in progress: what to do now

While you wait, protect the house and keep people away from sewage. Raw wastewater carries bacteria and should not be walked through or cleaned with bare hands.

  1. Stop water use. No showers, laundry or dishwasher. Every gallon goes toward the lowest opening.
  2. Keep out of the area. Keep children and pets away from standing wastewater.
  3. Move belongings up. Lift rugs, boxes and electronics off wet floors if it is safe.
  4. Call the 24/7 line. Tell us which drains are affected so we bring the right equipment.

Both tools have a place. The right choice depends on what is blocking the line.

We often start with a camera, because jetting through a broken line can make things worse. Our sewer inspection page explains what we look for.

What hydro jetting can and cannot do

Jetting works on cast iron, plastic and clay lines that are structurally sound. It will not repair a crack, fill a bellied section or fix a root-damaged joint. If the camera shows damage, we discuss repair options such as spot repair or lining first.

Roots are a middle case. A jetter with a root-cutting nozzle can clear fine roots and give the line a clean wall, but roots will grow back if the joint they entered is still open. We tell you what the camera shows and what we recommend.

Local note

Jetting and the lines under Lake Stevens

Homes built in the Lake Stevens growth years after 2000 mostly have plastic drain pipe, which jets cleanly and rarely corrodes. Older houses near downtown and the lake date back to the resort and commuter decades and can have cast iron or clay with rough walls that catch debris.

Whether the pipe in question belongs to you or to the Lake Stevens Sewer District depends on where it sits. The sewer district can tell you where its responsibility ends and yours begins before you authorize work on a line near the street.

Code and standards around jetting

The Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), adopted with state amendments in WAC 51-56, requires cleanouts at set points in a drain system, and those access points are where a jetter hose usually enters. A camera inspection before and after jetting documents the pipe condition so you can see what the work changed.

Questions we hear

Do you offer emergency hydro jetting at night or on weekends in Lake Stevens?

Yes. Our line answers 24/7. Tell us how many fixtures are backing up and whether a camera has inspected the line.

Is hydro jetting safe for old pipes?

It is safe on sound pipe. Cracked, collapsed or severely corroded pipe should be inspected first, and we use a camera to confirm.

How often should a line be jetted?

Only when the camera or repeat clogging says it is needed. Lines coated with grease may benefit from periodic jetting, while most homes do not need a schedule.

Will jetting damage my plumbing?

On a sound line, no. We choose pressure and nozzle for the pipe type and age.

Is jetting better than a cable?

For repeat clogs and grease buildup, yes. For a simple plug in a short branch line, a cable is faster and cheaper.
