Can a Water Softener Go Outside in Lake Stevens?

A water softener can go outside only if it is protected from freezing, sun and rain and the install meets code. In Lake Stevens, the better question is whether your water is hard enough to need one at all.

The short answer on outdoor softeners

Most softener manufacturers design their units for indoor use or for sheltered spaces. Outside, a softener faces three threats: freezing, direct sun and weather. Freezing can crack the resin tank, valve and brine tank. Sun can degrade plastic parts and warm the unit beyond its rated range. Rain and debris can foul the controls and brine.

Some models are rated for outdoor use, and some owners build an insulated, ventilated enclosure. If you must put one outside, check the manufacturer's temperature range, protect the plumbing from freezing and keep the electrical connection weatherproof. Garages, basements and utility rooms are better homes.

If you consider an outdoor install

  • Confirm the model is rated for outdoor use and check the temperature range.
  • Protect pipes and valves from freezing with insulation and a heated enclosure if needed.
  • Shade the unit and keep the brine tank dry.
  • Provide a drain for regeneration water that meets local rules.
  • Use a weatherproof outlet and a bypass valve for service.

Do you need a softener in Lake Stevens at all?

Snohomish County PUD lists total hardness for the Lake Stevens water system between 9 and 89 ppm, with an average of 73 ppm. That is roughly 4.3 grains per gallon on average, which many water quality scales rate as moderately soft to slightly hard. Many households in that range see little scale and choose not to soften.

Test before you buy. A simple hardness test strip from a hardware store shows where your tap falls. If you see white buildup on fixtures, spotty dishes or scale in the water heater, the number may be higher at your address than the average. You can also ask the PUD for its latest water quality report at 425-397-3000.

Local note

Local rules and conditions

Water softeners discharge brine during regeneration. Ask the Lake Stevens Sewer District how it handles softener discharge before you connect a drain, and do not send brine to a septic system or a storm drain without checking the rules.

Washington's state plumbing code, WAC 51-56, adopts the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) with state amendments, and a softener installation involves plumbing that may need a permit. Ask the City of Lake Stevens building department.

Lake Stevens grew from 6,361 residents in 2000 to 28,069 in 2010. Newer homes often have a plumbed-in loop or a spot for a softener near the water heater. Check the garage or utility area for a bypass tee or extra stub.

What a test result tells you

A hardness test gives you a number, and the number tells you what to do next. Under about 3 grains per gallon, most households see little effect. Between 3 and 7, some see spotting and light scale and many get by with simple maintenance. Above 7 grains, scale is more likely and a softener starts to look worth it. Your tap may differ from the PUD system average, so test at your own faucet.

A test also shows if you have other issues. Iron staining, a sulfur smell or cloudy water are not hardness problems, and a softener may not solve them. A lab test or a conversation with a water treatment specialist helps sort them out before you buy equipment.

If you decide a softener makes sense, size it from your actual hardness and your household water use rather than from a sales brochure. An oversized unit wastes salt and water, and an undersized one runs out of capacity between regenerations.

Alternatives to an outdoor softener

If your hardness is modest, you can manage scale without a softener. Flush the water heater yearly, clean aerators and showerheads and use a descaler on fixtures. Some homeowners add a point-of-use filter at the kitchen sink for taste.
